Orbit Census

Pick a space X and type a map f : X → X. Draw an arrow from every point x to f(x). Each connected piece of the resulting directed graph is a cycle with trees hanging off it, or a forward-infinite path that never closes up. This applet takes a census of the cycles: fixed points first, then cycles of length 2, 3, … up to the bound you set with periods ≤.

Cycles are colored by length. A filled dot is an attracting periodic point (the multiplier λ = f′(x0)⋯f′(xn−1) has |λ| < 1), a hollow dot is repelling. Click a row of the table, an orbit in the list, or a point in the picture to highlight it.

On ℝ and on an interval

The picture is a cobweb plot: the graph of f, the diagonal, and each cycle drawn as a closed staircase between them. Points of period n are roots of fn(x) − x. The applet evaluates this on a fine grid, brackets each sign change, polishes it by bisection, and also hunts tangential roots where the graph only touches the diagonal. It then sorts roots by their least period and merges points that belong to the same cycle. On ℝ the search window is whatever you see; drag and zoom to look elsewhere. On [a, b] the whole interval is searched, and cycles that leave it are discarded.

For a polynomial of degree d ≥ 2 the table also shows the most cycles a period can possibly have (see the count over ℂ below), so you can see when the real line has all of them, as for the logistic map at r = 4.

Sharkovsky's theorem

Order the positive integers like this:

3 ▷ 5 ▷ 7 ▷ 9 ▷ … ▷ 2·3 ▷ 2·5 ▷ … ▷ 4·3 ▷ 4·5 ▷ … ▷ 2k·3 ▷ … ▷ 23 ▷ 22 ▷ 2 ▷ 1

Odd numbers first, then twice the odds, then four times the odds, and so on, with the powers of 2 last, in decreasing order. Sharkovsky's theorem (1964): if a continuous map of an interval into ℝ has a point of least period m, it has points of least period n for every n with m ▷ n. In particular, period 3 forces every period (Li and Yorke rediscovered this in 1975 under the title Period three implies chaos). The theorem is sharp: for every m there is a continuous map whose set of periods is exactly {n : m ⊵ n}. The example pl(x, 1,3, 2,5, 3,4, 4,2, 5,1) is the classic one with period 5 and no period 3.

The proof only looks at the intervals between consecutive points of the given orbit and how f stretches them over each other. So it is enough that f is continuous on the smallest interval containing that orbit, and the forced orbits live inside that interval too. The applet uses exactly this: it takes the Sharkovsky-strongest period it found, checks numerically that f has no jumps or poles on the span of a witnessing orbit, and lights up every period that orbit forces, including periods beyond your search bound. Forced periods that the numerical search did not find are not errors in the theorem; they exist, usually in very thin windows.

A corollary worth knowing: if a continuous map has only finitely many periods, they are 1, 2, 4, …, 2k. The logistic map at r = 3.5 is an example.

On ℤ, ℤ/n and ℚ

Here arithmetic is exact (big integers and fractions), and the picture is the functional graph itself: cycles as rings, trees of preimages hanging off them.

ℤ/n. The formula is evaluated as an exact rational at x = 0, 1, …, n − 1 and reduced mod n (a denominator must be invertible mod n). Since X is finite, every orbit ends in a cycle and the census is complete. The panel also reports the size of the image f(X) and the number of points with no preimage.

ℤ. Every orbit starting in the window |x| ≤ M is followed exactly until it closes a cycle, joins an orbit already seen, or passes the escape bound. Every cycle that meets the window is therefore found. Points outside the window are not drawn; a dashed arrow labeled +k means the orbit spent k − 1 steps outside before coming back. Orbits that pass the escape bound all flow into one node, ∞. For a polynomial that is honest: ∞ is a fixed point of the map extended to the projective line. The basin column counts the starting points that end up in each cycle. The Collatz map has exactly five known cycles on ℤ, of lengths 1, 2, 3, 5 and 18, and the conjecture is that there are no others.

ℚ. The starting points are the fractions p/q with q up to the bound and |p/q| ≤ the window. For a polynomial with rational coefficients, Northcott's theorem says the rational periodic points have bounded height, so an orbit whose height blows up is genuinely wandering. For x2 + c one can have rational cycles of length 1, 2 and 3 (try c = −29/16). Poonen conjectured that no other lengths occur; lengths 4 and 5 are proven impossible, and length 6 is impossible under standard conjectures.

On ℂ

Periodic points are found with Newton's method applied to fn(z) − z, started from a grid filling the visible window. For a polynomial of degree d ≥ 2, fn(z) = z has dn solutions with multiplicity, and Möbius inversion gives the number with least period exactly n:

ν(n) = Σk | n μ(n/k) dk, so for d = 2: 2, 2, 6, 12, 30, 54, 126, 240, …

The table shows found / expected cycles (ν(n)/n), so you can see whether the hunt was complete. The background shades the escape time; the dark set is where orbits stay bounded (the filled Julia set, for a polynomial). Sharkovsky's theorem is a fact about the line and has no analogue here: z ↦ ωz with ω a primitive cube root of unity has the fixed point 0 and every other point has period 3, with no period 2 anywhere.

Writing f

Use x (or z), numbers, + − * / ^, implicit multiplication (3x, r x(1-x)), constants pi, e, i. Functions: sin cos tan exp log sqrt abs floor ceil round sign frac min max mod gcd, and the hyperbolic and inverse trig functions. a % b is the remainder in [0, b). Comparisons < <= == != >= > give 1 or 0, and if(cond, a, b) picks a branch. pl(x, x1,y1, x2,y2, …) is the piecewise-linear map through the given points. Single letters such as r, c, a, k become parameters with sliders; their boxes accept fractions like -29/16, which stay exact over ℤ and ℚ.

Fine print

On ℝ and ℂ everything is floating point. Roots are verified by their residual, discontinuities are detected numerically, and two cycles closer than about 10−8 are merged. On ℤ and ℚ only the explicitly listed operations are exact; a formula with sqrt or sin over ℤ falls back to floating point and must land on integers.
