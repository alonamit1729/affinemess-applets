How did Germany lose its edge in mathematics?

Judging a country’s level of excellence in mathematics by the number of Fields medal recipients is dubious (and I’ll be shocked if Germany doesn’t land another Fields at the next ICM*), but it cannot be denied that Germany’s position among countries in mathematical research is no longer what it was at the beginning of the 20th century.

Assigning cause and effect to such historical processes is often difficult, but in this case one key event can actually be pinpointed with reasonable reliability: the rise of the Nazis and the expulsion of Jewish scientists from German universities, particularly Göttingen, in the years leading to WWII.

In 1934, David Hilbert was sitting next to the Nazis' newly appointed minister of education [Bernhard Rust - AA], at a banquet. When asked, "And how is mathematics in Göttingen, now that it has been freed of the Jewish influence?" Hilbert replied: "Mathematics in Göttingen? There is really no such thing any more."[1]

The name “Göttingen” doesn’t mean much to most people nowadays, but mathematicians are well aware of the superstars who did their chief work there: Gauss, Riemann, Dirichlet, Hilbert, Klein, Noether, Weyl. Such a succession of luminaries did not occur, as far as I can tell, anywhere else before or after, and it was all set to continue this illustrious trajectory into the late 20th century. But during the 1930’s, Göttingen died.

In 1933, the Nazis began to systematically drive Jewish mathematicians and scientists away from Germany. Among those directly affected were physicists Max Born, Leo Szilard, Edward Teller, Hans Bethe and another one whose name escapes me right now. Ah, yes: Albert Einstein. In mathematics, suffice it to mention the names of Edmund Landau, Emmy Noether, Felix Bernstein, Richard Brauer, Eugene Wigner, Felix Hausdorff, Hermann Weyl (whose wife was Jewish), Max Dehn (who survived a bit longer than the others, but eventually was forced to flee), Richard Courant, and of course, John von Neumann. For this man alone to move from one country to another could alter the course of history.

Göttingen never regained anything close to its former prominence, and neither did Germany. Hasse and Siegel, two mathematical luminaries, tried to resurrect it, but failed. (Hasse was a right-wing nationalist, but he was denied a membership in the Nazi party because he actually had Jewish roots).

And who knows, if not for the Nazis, Grothendieck himself may have remained a German.


* Added later: I wrote that because I expected Peter Scholze to be a laureate in 2018, and indeed he was.
