Five-point shape strata

What the solution sets behind the five-point row of the fixed-distances table look like: the ambient shape space, the incidence graphs of the one-parameter families, the degeneration poset of all strata, and how each number was computed. Companion to Planar sets with a fixed number of distances; data and scripts in tools/fixed-distances/ of the applets repository.

The ambient space

Five points in the plane up to similarity have 2·5 − 4 = 6 degrees of freedom. The natural compact model is Kendall's shape space: labelled configurations with coincidences allowed (but not all five points equal), up to orientation-preserving similarity, form ℂP³; the collision loci zᵢ = zⱼ are its ten hyperplanes. Removing them leaves M₀,₆, the moduli space of six distinct points on the sphere with one at ∞. Quotienting by relabelling (S₅) and by reflections (complex conjugation) gives the unlabelled space the table is about. Rational Betti numbers:

The invariants were computed on the Orlik–Solomon algebra of the braid arrangement (check: F(ℂ,5)/S₅ has the cohomology of a circle, Arnold's theorem). The orbifold points of the unlabelled space are the configurations with a symmetry; the mirror locus has codimension 3, so the one-parameter families below never fold.

Each family is one realizable colouring type of K₅ (which pairs are at equal distance), and its dimension is that of its real solution set modulo similarity. The stratification question is how these pieces are glued: where a family's closure meets the strata with fewer distances.

The simpler cases: three and four points

Three points. The shape space of triangles (unlabelled, reflections allowed) is a closed curvilinear triangle: the quotient of Kendall's sphere by the 12 symmetries. Its strata are exactly the table's (3,1), (3,2), (3,3): the equilateral triangle is a corner, the isosceles triangles form two sides meeting there (apex angle running from 60° up to 180° on one side and down to 0° on the other), and the scalene triangles are the interior together with the third side, the collinear triples, which is a mirror of the orbifold rather than a boundary of the stratum. One corner is a collision and not part of the space; the other, the collinear triple with the apex at the midpoint, is where the isosceles family folds: the family meets its own reflection and turns back.

Four points. The ambient space is four-dimensional (ℂP²/conj is a 4-sphere; its quotient by S₄ is the orbifold the table lives in). The six two-distance quadruples are the rigid points, the nine three-distance families are curves, the seven four-distance families surfaces, and the two five-distance families and the generic quadruples fill the rest. Every curve bounds at least one surface, and "D on the unit circle about a vertex of an equilateral triangle" (family 1) bounds five of the seven.

So the pattern that the five-point computation found is already visible here: rigid sets are the vertices, each one-parameter family is a few arcs between them with ends running off to collisions, the graph is connected, and the only new ingredient at n = 5 is the junction (a curve ending in the interior of a lower family), which cannot happen for n ≤ 4 because the four-distance-and-lower families there are the rigid points and the curves of the same picture.

Method, in one paragraph each

Families. For each colouring type the squared-distance equations with the scale fixed, saturated by the inequations (all values distinct and nonzero) in Singular, give the closure of the family; the certification of the counts (msolve real root isolation, primary decomposition, hidden-point and node tests) is described in the applet's About text.

Boundary points and arcs. Adding one coincidence equation to a curve's ideal gives a zero-dimensional system; msolve isolates its real roots to 200 bits and each root is matched up to similarity against the certified rigid lists. The real curve is then traced numerically from every root (tangent step and Newton projection; arrival detected by proximity to the exact roots; escape when coordinates exceed 300) and from random slice points to catch closed loops (none). Traversals are grouped into geometric arcs by a permutation- and similarity-invariant fingerprint of the shapes along the arc, which collapses the two directions and all symmetric copies of a labelled arc into one.

Poset. Adding one coincidence equation to a higher-dimensional family's ideal gives its boundary; it was decomposed into irreducible components over ℚ (minAssGTZ, with a timeout) and each component's type read off a certified real point. Where the decomposition did not finish (most of d = 6, all of d = 7) random slices through the boundary supplied certified real points instead.

Caveats. Vertex–vertex arc counts are exact up to the few arcs whose two traversals did not pair (17 of 5269 for five distances); collision-end counts are approximate (±40). Type 327 of (5,5) has 5 lost traces. Sampled poset data lists only strata that were hit, and small-integer slices hit symmetric special points with positive probability, so a sampled list can include a corner alongside the genuine boundary faces. The three (5,5) types with both a curve and a surface were traced on their curve components only.
