Planar sets with a fixed number of distances

Take n points in the plane and count the distinct distances between them, d. The table shows, for each pair (n, d), how many configurations there are up to similarity. Click a cell to see them.

Reading a cell

Each line of a cell is one dimension of the solution set. 0d: 34 means 34 rigid configurations; 1d: 27 means 27 one-parameter families; 2d: [3, 20] means between 3 and 20 two-parameter families; ≥ 3 means no upper bound is known. A family is a realizable pattern of which pairs of points are at equal distance (an edge colouring of Kn up to symmetry), and its dimension is that of its solution set modulo similarity, measured in the full configuration space. Solid cells are known exactly; dashed cells carry bounds from constructed examples, each verified numerically to be isolated or to lie on a family of the stated dimension; a dimension that is not listed simply has no known example (and is excluded where a cell is marked finite). The ochre cell is the open case, where it is not known whether any configuration exists. Blank cells are impossible.

Marks: * the count comes from our own numerical computation and we found nothing published for it; † a published classification exists but the number shown is from our computation and was not checked against the paper; ‡ the count is certified by exact computation: for every colouring type the polynomial system was solved symbolically, so unrealizable types are proved unrealizable, rigid configurations are isolated as real algebraic numbers, and each positive-dimensional component was decomposed and tested for real points.

Why the dimensions

Modulo similarity, n points have 2n − 4 degrees of freedom and C(n,2) distances. Asking for exactly d values imposes C(n,2) − d coincidences, so one expects a solution set of dimension 2n − 4 − C(n,2) + d. No component can be smaller than that, but symmetric configurations give bigger ones, which is why some cells mix rigid configurations with families (three and four points are the only cases where that never happens).

What is known

The maximum number of points for d distances is g(d) = 3, 5, 7, 9, 12, 13 for d = 1, …, 6 (Erdős–Fishburn 1996 for d ≤ 4, Shinohara 2008 for 5, Wei 2012 for 6), with the extremal configurations classified. For d = 7 a 16-point example is known and nothing larger; the general bound gives at most 36. Two-distance sets were classified by Einhorn and Schoenberg (1966), three-distance sets with at least five points by Shinohara (2004).

For the dashed cells the lower bounds come from explicit constructions: subsets of regular polygons, pieces of the triangular lattice, one-point extensions of the smaller complete lists, and a systematic search over configurations with a cyclic or dihedral symmetry (orbit structures with up to five parameters and up to two extra coincidences). Every configuration in a catalog was checked numerically to have exactly the stated number of distances and the stated local dimension. Upper bounds are shown only where a classification or a finiteness argument exists: Lan and Wei (2013) count 42 seven-point four-distance sets; eight-point four-distance sets are finite because they extend a seven-point set with three or four distances by a point with finitely many positions; Wei (2011) classifies eleven-point five-distance sets.

The five-point row with four to seven distances is certified (‡). For each colouring type of K₅ the squared-distance equations, with the scale fixed and the inequations “all values distinct and nonzero” imposed through a Rabinowitsch variable, were handed to msolve: an empty Gröbner basis proves the type unrealizable, a zero-dimensional system yields its real solutions with certified isolating intervals, and a positive-dimensional one is decomposed into minimal primes with Singular, each prime checked for degeneracy and probed for real points by random hyperplane slices; real-isolated points of the curves were excluded by checking that every curve is smooth. For 21 of the 768 types with five to seven distances the primary decomposition did not finish; there the certificate is the dimension of the saturated ideal (a Gröbner basis computation, which shows every component has the expected dimension), certified real points on random slices, and full rank of the Jacobian at those points, so they lie on smooth components of that dimension. This corrected the numerical search in both directions: for five points and four distances it had found 468 of the 481 rigid configurations, had reported two rigid types as families, and had called the equally spaced collinear quintuple rigid when it in fact lies on a one-parameter family.

The cells for four points, and the counts 34 and 9 for five and six points with three distances, come from a numerical computation made for this applet (October 2026): every colouring of the edges of Kn with d colours up to symmetry was tested for realizability by random-start least squares, the dimension of the solution set was measured at each solution, and rigid solutions were clustered by congruence. For five points with four distances the run was repeated with a second random seed, and the 34 five-point three-distance sets were also obtained by a second, independent method (extending the four-point families). The fourth nine-point four-distance set, which Erdős and Fishburn count but which we could not find described anywhere accessible, was recovered by a search over mirror-symmetric configurations. These counts are numerical and have not been proved by hand; the lists are exhaustive only up to the reliability of a random search.

Flexing

Tinted cards are families. Hovering (or tapping, on a touch screen) makes the configuration move along its family: at each frame the tangent space of the solution set is computed from the Jacobian of the equal-distance equations, the configuration takes a small step along it and is projected back onto the constraint set by a few Newton iterations, so the equalities hold exactly throughout. One-parameter families glide back and forth along their curve, turning around before two points merge or two distance values coincide, so the motion loops; higher-dimensional families wander within the same bounds. Only one card moves at a time; tapping a moving card restarts it from its resting shape. Where branches of a family cross, the motion may switch branches.

Coordinates

Each catalog entry has a coordinate list in a natural frame: a regular-polygon frame (circumradius 1) or a lattice frame when the configuration has one, otherwise the shortest distance is 1 with the first point at the origin. Exact expressions are given where the frame allows; clicking the list copies the decimal coordinates as JSON.
