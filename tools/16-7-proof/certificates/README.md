# Certificates for discarded patterns

The proof in `../two-shapes.ipynb` uses one number from the literature: at most 228 distance patterns
occur for ten points in the plane with at most six distances (Szöllősi and Östergård, Table 9). That
number comes out of a generation, level by level from four to ten points, in which candidates are
*discarded* when a system of polynomial equations has no admissible solution.

This folder derives the 228 again, with a certificate for every discarded candidate: a polynomial
identity that can be checked by multiplying out, and nothing else.

A *candidate* on n points is a pattern with at most six colours all of whose (n-1)-point sub-patterns are
kept at the level below. Every number in the table equals the corresponding number in Table 9 of Szöllősi
and Östergård, and the 228 patterns at ten points are the 228 of `../data/L10.json`.

All 527,338 certificates pass `check.py`, both when they were made and again as the final files. From eight
points on nothing needs to be discarded, and the search finds no certificate (with a product of degree up
to 5) for any of the 2,428 candidates there.

## What a certificate is

Take a pattern on n points with colours 0..k-1, and suppose points of the plane have it, with squared
distances x_0, ..., x_{k-1} (positive and pairwise different). For a base point b put

    G_ij = x_c(b,i) + x_c(b,j) - x_c(i,j),        G_ii = 2 x_c(b,i)        (i, j different from b)

where c(i,j) is the colour of the pair. This is twice the Gram matrix of the vectors p_i - p_b, which lie
in a plane, so every 3x3 minor of G is zero. The minors are cubic polynomials in the x.

A certificate is a polynomial identity

    scale * (a product of factors x_c and x_a - x_b)  =  sum of (polynomial) * (minor)

with a non-zero integer `scale`. At the point x the right side is zero and the left side is not, so no
such points exist. Example, the first five-point certificate:

    18 x_0^3  =  13 M_0(123;123) - 3 M_1(234;234) - 22 M_0(123;124)

where M_b(rows;columns) is the minor of G for the base point b. The "degree of the product" in the table
is the number of factors on the left; for degree 3 the multipliers are plain integers, as here.

`check.py` checks such identities. It is about seventy lines of pure Python, uses integers only, and
recomputes the minors from the pattern itself. It is the only code here that has to be trusted for the
certificates.

## Checking

The five-point certificates are in this folder. The six- and seven-point ones are too large for the
repository (1.2 GB as text) and are attached to the release `certificates-v1`:

    https://github.com/alonamit1729/affinemess-applets/releases/download/certificates-v1/level6.jsonl.gz
    https://github.com/alonamit1729/affinemess-applets/releases/download/certificates-v1/level7.jsonl.gz

    python3 check.py --jobs 4 level5.jsonl level6.jsonl.gz level7.jsonl.gz

This takes about 35 core-minutes. `SHA256SUMS` lists the checksums of the three files.

## What this does and does not establish

* Each certificate proves that its pattern is not the pattern of any planar point set. That part needs
  only `check.py`.
* That the candidates at each level are *all* the patterns whose sub-patterns are kept is combinatorics:
  an exhaustive generation. Five points was generated by `fewdist.add_a_point` (one folder up); six to ten
  points by `tools/fixed-distances/g7fin/gen/gen.py` (canonical forms by nauty). The counts agree with
  the published table at every level. A second, independent generation of levels six to ten is still to
  be done.
* The notebook still quotes the number 228 from the literature. Rewriting its Proposition A to rest on
  this folder instead is the next step.

How the certificates were found does not matter for their validity. For the record: `fast.py` computes
the minors, works modulo a prime with the degree-d part of the ideal they generate, tests *all* products
of d forbidden factors against it (d = 3, 4, 5), and then solves for the multipliers exactly. On the
kept side, `kept_check.py` was run on a sample at six points (see the table of files).

## Files

| file | what it is |
|---|---|
| `check.py` | the checker |
| `level5.jsonl` | 272 certificates: the one-coloured four-point pattern and the 271 discarded five-point candidates |
| `L5.json`, `L6.json`, `L7.json` | the patterns kept at five, six and seven points (922, 20,229, 4,667) |
| `L8.json`, `L9.json`, `L10.json` | the candidates at eight, nine and ten points (1,565, 635, 228), all kept |
| `SHA256SUMS` | checksums of `level5.jsonl`, `level6.jsonl.gz`, `level7.jsonl.gz` |
| `fast.py`, `find.py` | the search (numpy, python-flint); `find.py` is the first version, used by the pilot |
| `run_level5.py` | five points, start to finish |
| `run_level.py`, `finish_level.py` | a whole level in resumable chunks, and collecting it |
| `pilot6_sample.json`, `pilot6.jsonl.gz`, `pilot6_kept.json`, `run_pilot6.py` | the pilot: 1,060 random six-point candidates |
| `kept_check.py` | a check on the kept side, not part of any proof: for the 50 kept candidates of the pilot there is, modulo the prime, a solution of the equations at which no forbidden factor vanishes |

A certificate is one JSON object per line: `n`, `pattern`, `scale`, `product` (factors `[c]` for x_c or
`[a, b]` for x_a - x_b, each with its exponent) and `terms` (base point, rows, columns, and the multiplier
as a list of `[coefficient, exponents]`).

## Running it again

    python3 run_level5.py                                                                     # 8 minutes
    python3 run_level.py 6 raw6.json run6 && python3 finish_level.py 6 run6 raw6.json out     # 4.5 core-hours
    python3 run_level.py 7 raw7.json run7 && python3 finish_level.py 7 run7 raw7.json out     # 20 core-minutes

`raw6.json` and `raw7.json` are the candidate lists written by `gen.py --gen-only` from `L5.json` and
`L6.json`. The runs are deterministic.
