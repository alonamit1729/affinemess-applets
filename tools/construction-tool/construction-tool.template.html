Construction Tool

How many points can you put in the plane so that only d different distances occur between them? Five points can have just two distances (the regular pentagon), seven points need three, and sixteen points need seven. This tool lets you build such sets with straightedge, compass and a few friends, keeps the ones you find, and shows how every set in the catalog can be built. (It used to be called Distance Hunt; a game of that name may come back later.)

Catalog cards

Every card of the n points, d distances catalog opens here as a construction. The card shows the set; Show construction draws the lines and circles, Play builds it step by step (or step with ‹ ›, or click a step in the list), and Edit makes it your own construction, with every tool, so you can change it or build on it (Undo brings back what you had). ‹ › in the top bar move through the cell's cards.

Each construction uses the simplest tools that the search found, in this order: a piece of the triangular or square lattice (nothing to construct); straightedge and compass (a regular polygon counts when straightedge and compass can draw it, as for 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 17, 20, 24 or 30 sides); straightedge, compass and a regular polygon of another kind (7, 9, 11, 13, 14, 18 or 36 sides); and, when nothing else was found, sliders and free points pinned by equalities. A construction found shows that a set needs at most those tools; where the Galois analysis is known, the card also says what is actually needed. The search uses only points of the set (and of its starting lattice or polygon) as stepping stones, so for some sets that straightedge and compass could build, it settles for an equality.

Sets that flex (families) are built with free points: a diamond is a slider, locked to its line or circle; a ring is a point free in the whole plane. They swing by themselves to show the family, and you can drag them: the distances that are equal stay equal.

Building your own

  • You start with two points, a unit apart. Build with the tools on the left; every intersection of two lines or circles becomes a small hollow dot you can use.
  • Mark the points that belong to your set: hold the mouse on a point for a moment, with any tool, or Shift-click it (free points are marked when you drop them). With Select (Esc) a plain click marks too. Do the same again to unmark. The panel on the right counts them, n, and the distinct distances between them, d, and colours each segment by its length. Lengths are in units of the starting segment. Hover a length in the panel to bring out its segments, or hover a segment to find its length; click a length to keep it highlighted.
  • When you like what you see, press Keep. Similar sets (same shape at any size, rotation or reflection) count once, so the collection only grows when you find something genuinely new. The small link button in a card's corner copies a link that opens Distance Hunt with that set on the canvas, to share or to come back to.
  • The two Lattice buttons, triangular (G) and square (Q), reveal the lattice built on the two starting points (one at a time). Lattice points work like any other point, and since similar sets count as the same, every lattice piece can be found on these two.

The tools

  • Line through two points; Parallel and Perpendicular: pick a line (or a segment between two marked points) and a point; Bisector, the perpendicular bisector of two points. Hover a tool to see what it does.
  • Circle: a centre, then a point on the circle. Compass: two points for the radius, then the centre.
  • Polygon: a regular polygon with the chosen number of sides on a segment, together with its centre. It sits to the left of the segment as you pick it; pick the ends the other way round for the other side.
  • Free point: click on a line or circle for a slider, a point that moves only along it, drawn as a diamond; click anywhere else for a point that moves freely in the plane, drawn as a ring. They are not exact until equalities pin them down (then they fill in); see below.
  • Equal: click a segment of your set, then another; the tool moves the free points and sliders so that the two have the same length, and marks equal segments with matching ticks.
  • The Show buttons: Construction (H) hides the lines, circles, polygons and unmarked points, leaving the set and its coloured distances; picking a drawing tool brings them back. All distances (E) colours every segment between marked points (for sets of up to 24 points); Shortest distances (M) draws only the segments of the shortest length, which makes the structure of a big set, a piece of lattice say, easy to see. The corner readout shows coordinates, in units of the starting segment, of the pointer or of the point under it.

With sliders and equalities (below), every set in the catalog of the n points, d distances explorer can be built; each catalog card shows one way.

Sliders and equalities

Some sets cannot be constructed at all. For 75 of the 468 rigid five-point four-distance sets, the distances solve equations whose Galois groups (S₅, S₆, S₇) are not solvable, so no straightedge, compass, angle divider or regular polygon will ever reach them.

For those, say what you want and let the tool solve it. Put a slider on a line or circle, build the rest of the set from it (circles and intersections follow the slider as it moves), then use Equal to ask for two segments to have the same length. A slider has one unknown and an equality is one equation, so the slider is pinned: the game finds every position along its line or circle where the two lengths agree, moves there, and shows the others as blue rings you can click. A free point has two unknowns and needs two equalities; several sliders and free points can be solved together. Until everything is pinned, dragging keeps the equalities you asked for. The panel counts equations and unknowns, and lists the equalities (hover one to see its segments, × to remove it).

One slider and one equality are enough for all but three of the catalog's sets that ordinary construction cannot reach; the remaining three ((5,4) nos. 461, 463 and 466) need three sliders solved together. Pinned points are exact to machine precision rather than by construction, and their cards say snapped.

The catalog

Every set is compared with the 59,382 rigid sets catalogued in the explorer (the four largest cells, (6,5), (6,6), (7,6) and (8,6), are fetched when a rigid set first appears there; (6,6) alone holds 44,242 sets, about 8 MB). A matching card shows the catalog's number and name (the numbers are the explorer's card numbers in that cell), and each (n, d) heading shows how many of that cell's rigid sets you have found; "+" marks cells whose list is not known to be complete.

The panel also says what it takes to build a catalog set. Most need only straightedge and compass. Some need a regular 7-, 9-, 11-, 13-, 14- or 18-gon (the Polygon tool supplies it), 58 need angle trisection, 32 lead to quartics with Galois group S₄ that straightedge and compass cannot solve, and 75 cannot be constructed by any of these means: their distances solve equations with Galois group S₅, S₆ or S₇, or need the real root of a cubic with one real root. The verdicts come from the minimal polynomials of the squared distance ratios, computed to 1,300 digits, and their Galois groups. They cover the 2,298 sets the catalog held on 3 November 2026; sets added since then show no verdict yet. A catalog number opens that card, with its construction, in a new tab. Sets that can still flex are kept too, marked flexible; they belong to families rather than to the list of rigid sets.

Saving

No account needed: your collection and your current construction live in this browser. Browsers can clear that storage (Safari after about seven days without a visit, and private windows forget on closing), so use Save & share to download a file or make a link that carries the whole collection. Loading a file or a link merges it with what you have.
