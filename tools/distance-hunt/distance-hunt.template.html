Distance Hunt

How many points can you put in the plane so that only d different distances occur between them? Five points can have just two distances (the regular pentagon), seven points need three, and sixteen points need seven. This game lets you hunt for such sets with straightedge, compass and a few friends, and keeps the ones you find.

How to play

  • You start with two points, a unit apart. Build with the tools on the left; every intersection of two lines or circles becomes a small hollow dot you can use.
  • Mark the points that belong to your set: hold the mouse on a point for a moment, with any tool, or Shift-click it (free points are marked when you drop them). With Select (Esc) a plain click marks too. Do the same again to unmark. The panel on the right counts them, n, and the distinct distances between them, d, and colours each segment by its length. Lengths are in units of the starting segment. Hover a length in the panel to bring out its segments, or hover a segment to find its length; click a length to keep it highlighted.
  • When you like what you see, press Keep. Similar sets (same shape at any size, rotation or reflection) count once, so the collection only grows when you find something genuinely new.
  • The Triangular and Square buttons reveal the lattice built on the two starting points (one at a time). Lattice points work like any other point, and since similar sets count as the same, every lattice piece can be found on these two.

The tools

  • Line through two points; Parallel and Perpendicular: pick a line (or a segment between two marked points) and a point; Bisector, the perpendicular bisector of two points. Hover a tool to see what it does.
  • Circle: a centre, then a point on the circle. Compass: two points for the radius, then the centre.
  • Polygon: a regular polygon with the chosen number of sides on a segment, together with its centre. It sits to the left of the segment as you pick it; pick the ends the other way round for the other side.
  • Free point: click on a line or circle for a slider, a point that moves only along it; click anywhere else for a point that moves freely. Both are drawn as diamonds (a slider with a bar along its curve). They are not exact until equalities pin them down; see below.
  • Equal: click a segment of your set, then another; the game moves the free points and sliders so that the two have the same length, and marks equal segments with matching ticks.
  • Construction (H) hides the lines, circles, polygons and unmarked points, leaving the set and its coloured distances; picking a drawing tool brings them back. The corner readout shows coordinates, in units of the starting segment, of the pointer or of the point under it.

That is enough: every rigid set in the catalog of the n points, d distances explorer except a couple of hundred, nearly all of them five points with four distances, can be built this way.

Sliders and equalities

Some sets cannot be constructed at all. For 75 of the 468 rigid five-point four-distance sets, the distances solve equations whose Galois groups (S₅, S₆, S₇) are not solvable, so no straightedge, compass, angle divider or regular polygon will ever reach them.

For those, say what you want and let the game solve it. Put a slider on a line or circle, build the rest of the set from it (circles and intersections follow the slider as it moves), then use Equal to ask for two segments to have the same length. A slider has one unknown and an equality is one equation, so the slider is pinned: the game finds every position along its line or circle where the two lengths agree, moves there, and shows the others as blue rings you can click. A free point has two unknowns and needs two equalities; several sliders and free points can be solved together. Until everything is pinned, dragging keeps the equalities you asked for. The panel counts equations and unknowns, and lists the equalities (hover one to see its segments, × to remove it).

One slider and one equality are enough for all but three of the catalog's sets that ordinary construction cannot reach; the remaining three ((5,4) nos. 461, 463 and 466) need three sliders solved together. Pinned points are exact to machine precision rather than by construction, and their cards say snapped.

The catalog

Every set is compared with the 59,377 rigid sets catalogued in the explorer (the four largest cells, (6,5), (6,6), (7,6) and (8,6), are fetched when a rigid set first appears there; (6,6) alone holds 44,242 sets, about 8 MB). A matching card shows the catalog's number and name (the numbers are the explorer's card numbers in that cell), and each (n, d) heading shows how many of that cell's rigid sets you have found; "+" marks cells whose list is not known to be complete.

The panel also says what it takes to build a catalog set. Most need only straightedge and compass. Some need a regular 7-, 9-, 11-, 13-, 14- or 18-gon (the Polygon tool supplies it), 58 need angle trisection, 32 lead to quartics with Galois group S₄ that straightedge and compass cannot solve, and 75 cannot be constructed by any of these means: their distances solve equations with Galois group S₅, S₆ or S₇, or need the real root of a cubic with one real root. The verdicts come from the minimal polynomials of the squared distance ratios, computed to 1,300 digits, and their Galois groups. They cover the 2,298 sets the catalog held on 3 November 2026; sets added since then show no verdict yet. A catalog number links to that cell of the explorer. Sets that can still flex are kept too, marked flexible; they belong to families rather than to the list of rigid sets.

Saving

No account needed: your collection and your current construction live in this browser. Browsers can clear that storage (Safari after about seven days without a visit, and private windows forget on closing), so use Save & share to download a file or make a link that carries the whole collection. Loading a file or a link merges it with what you have.
