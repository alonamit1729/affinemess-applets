Why it looks self-similar

Since π is irrational, n mod π is equidistributed, so the values tan n are distributed like the tangent of a uniformly random angle: a standard Cauchy distribution. A sum of N independent Cauchy variables is again Cauchy, scaled by N: no averaging out, no √N. So, heuristically, f behaves like a Cauchy process, which is self-similar with exponent 1. Stretch the horizontal axis by λ and the typical vertical size grows by λ as well, which is why an auto-scaled window looks alike at every scale. It is only a heuristic: the terms are deterministic and highly correlated, and the correlations are exactly what makes the picture interesting.

Where the cliffs come from

|tan n| is large when n is close to an odd multiple of π/2, that is, when 2n/(2m+1) is an unusually good approximation of π. The record values of |tan n| line up with convergents and semiconvergents of π that have odd denominators:

Staircases

Because 355 ≈ 113π (off by 3.0·10−5), tan(n+355) is almost tan n, with the angle nudged slightly toward the pole. Starting from n = 344 the terms at 344, 699, 1054, … all sit just below a pole, with values 227.5, 229.1, 230.7, … creeping upward, and the partial sums climb a long staircase. After 146 steps, at n = 52,174, the angle crosses the pole, tan flips to −181,570, and the whole climb is undone in a single term. See it on [1, 60,000].

The same thing happens one level up with 5,419,351 ≈ 1,725,033π: large positive terms at 4,846,147, 10,265,498, …, 37,362,253 build the climb across the full range, and the term at 42,781,604 brings it down. See [1, 108]. Zoom far in anywhere and the near-periods 22 ≈ 7π and 355 ≈ 113π show up as approximate repetitions: [1, 400].

Beyond 108

Between 108 and 109 the picture is dominated by two convergents of π, and this time both terms are positive: tan 122,925,461 ≈ 3.27·108 and tan 534,483,448 ≈ 1.91·109, a single term more than three times its own index. They lift the curve to f(109) ≈ 1.626·109. See [1, 109].

How big can tan n be?

If |n − (2m+1)π/2| = δ then |tan n| ≈ 1/δ, so the question is how well π can be approximated by rationals. With the irrationality measure bound μ(π) ≤ 7.103 (Zeilberger and Zudilin, 2020), one gets |tan n| < n6.11 for all large n. If, as expected, μ(π) = 2, then |tan n| = O(n1+ε), consistent with the table above.

How it is computed

Every term is computed once, in background threads, using compensated (Neumaier) summation in double precision; JavaScript's Math.tan reduces integer arguments modulo π accurately. The applet stores f at every 256th N, plus the minimum and maximum of f within each block of 256: about 6 MB for 108 and 60 MB for 109. Windows up to 400,000 wide are recomputed term by term from the nearest checkpoint, so every plotted point is exact; wider windows are drawn from the block extremes, which still show every spike. An independent extended-precision computation agrees to about 15 significant digits; for instance f(108) = −55,500,322.5230… On a typical laptop 108 takes a few seconds and 109 roughly ten times as long; anything already computed can be explored right away.
